Interview 2 · Semi-Structured · Tom, 42

Interviewer used core questions with probes. Raw notes below, written live during the conversation.

• Chose neighborhood 9 yrs ago for schools + parks. "Good family neighborhood," would recommend it.

• Asked what a typical week looks like — mentioned working from home, kids at the elementary school two blocks over.

• Probe: "has anything changed in how you get around because of safety?" — YES. Changed kids' walking route to school. Reason: speeding cars at one crossing + a car theft nearby recently.

• Probe: "what led to that change specifically?" — said the car theft was "the last straw," had been thinking about it since the near-miss with speeding cars.

• Mentioned neighborhood social media app makes him "more worried than what I actually see day to day."

• Went to one police-district community meeting. Felt the information was "too general to actually help."

• Did not bring up police contact or reporting anything himself.

Interview 3 · Unstructured · Rosa, 67

Interviewer opened with one prompt and followed her lead. Raw notes below.

• Retired teacher, lived here 40 years. Talked a long time about how the block has changed over the decades — says she "loves living here" despite the changes.

• Brought up her husband unprompted — since he passed, she feels less safe being home alone at night. Added a doorbell camera and extra porch lights afterward.

• Reflected back: "so it sounds like it changed after that." She agreed, said it wasn't really about the neighborhood changing, more about being alone.

• Said a lot of longtime neighbors have moved away over the years. Misses knowing everyone — called it "eyes on the street," said that used to make her feel safe automatically.

• Volunteers at the community center now. Said she feels safest "around people I know," and that's part of why she keeps volunteering.

• Long pause after being asked what would make her feel safer today — eventually said "just more people like the old days," not any particular security measure.

2. Label

Pull at least 8 specific details from the notes above, from more than one interview. A detail worth pulling is something concrete: a decision someone made, a reason they gave, a feeling they described, or an event they mentioned. Skip plain facts like an age or a date; those don't code into anything interesting on their own.

For each detail, write a short label: a few words naming what that detail is an example of, not a restatement of the quote itself. A label should be something you could imagine applying to a similar detail from a completely different interview.

Weak vs. strong label: if a note says "started parking two blocks away after a car break-in," a weak label just repeats it "parking two blocks away." A stronger label names the pattern it's an example of: "changed routine after an incident." The stronger label is reusable; it would still fit if a different person changed a different routine for a similar reason.

3. Group Into Themes

Set the original notes aside for a moment and look only at your list of labels. Which ones are really pointing at the same underlying idea, even if the specific details are different? Group those into 2 or 3 themes. A useful test: if you described the theme to someone else, would each label in that group be a recognizable example of it?

Minimum requirement: at least 2 themes, each with a name, a definition, and supporting evidence. You may add a third.
What each field is looking for:
  • Theme name — a short phrase (3–6 words), not a full sentence. "Distrust of formal reporting" rather than "People don't trust reporting to help."
  • One-sentence definition — describe the pattern the theme captures, in your own words. This should say more than the name alone; someone who has not read your labels should understand what belongs in this theme and what does not.
  • Supporting evidence — the single strongest quote or detail from the notes above. Specific enough that another person could look at it and see exactly why it fits this theme.
